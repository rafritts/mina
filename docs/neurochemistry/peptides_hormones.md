# Peptides and Hormones: Oxytocin, Endorphins, Cortisol, Ghrelin, Orexin

Neuropeptides and steroid/peptide hormones. Unlike monoamines, most are not recycled via reuptake transporters — they are degraded by extracellular peptidases or metabolized by enzymes. Stored in large dense-core vesicles (LDCVs) and require high-frequency firing for release (except steroids, which are synthesized on demand).

---

## OXYTOCIN (OXT)

### Class and Type
Nonapeptide (9-amino acid) neuropeptide hormone. Member of the neurohypophysial hormone family, closely related to vasopressin (AVP; ~80% receptor-level sequence homology). Functions as both:
- A **hormone** (released into blood from posterior pituitary)
- A **neuromodulator/neurotransmitter** (released centrally from dendrites and axons via volume transmission)

### Where Synthesized
- **Primary CNS**: Magnocellular neurosecretory neurons in the **paraventricular nucleus (PVN)** and **supraoptic nucleus (SON)** of the hypothalamus. Accessory hypothalamic nuclei contribute minor amounts.
- **Peripheral**: Uterus, placenta, corpus luteum, testes, heart, thymus. These peripheral sources contribute to local autocrine/paracrine action but not meaningfully to plasma levels under normal conditions.

### Where Stored
- **Posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis)**: LDCVs at axon terminals — Herring bodies — for peripheral release into blood.
- **Hypothalamic dendrites**: separate pool for central/local volume transmission release; independent of posterior pituitary.

### Release Dynamics
**Peripheral (blood)**:
- Pulsatile secretion from posterior pituitary; ~5–10 min pulses during breastfeeding.
- Triggered by: suckling reflex, uterine distension, social/affiliative stimuli.

**Central (brain)**:
- Somatodendritic release from PVN/SON dendrites — floods nearby CSF/extracellular space.
- Can be independent of peripheral release.
- Stress, social interaction, and sensory bonding stimuli can trigger central release.

**Tonic baseline**: Low-level tonic release maintains social vigilance and pair-bond stability.

### Units of Measurement
- Plasma: **pg/mL** (or pmol/L; 1 pg/mL ≈ 0.91 pmol/L; MW ~1007 Da)
- CSF: **pg/mL**

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

| Compartment | Concentration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Plasma (extracted assay) | 1–10 pg/mL; mean ~4.75 pg/mL | Unextracted assays return spuriously high values (up to 358 pg/mL) due to matrix interference — major methodological problem |
| CSF (lumbar) | ~17–19 pg/mL | More reflective of central release than plasma; meta-analytic estimate ~17.3 pg/mL |
| Plasma during childbirth (parturition) | ~100–300 pg/mL | Sustained pulsatile discharge |
| Plasma during breastfeeding (per pulse) | ~3–10× over baseline | Individual pulses |
| Plasma after peak bonding | ~50–100% above resting baseline | Short-term spike |
| Plasma after social touch/eye contact | ~10–30% above resting | Highly variable |
| CSF after intranasal OXT (24 IU) | ~2–5× basal CSF level | Peaks at +45–75 min |

**Note**: Plasma OXT and CSF OXT do **not** correlate under basal conditions — they reflect two independent secretory pools.

### Blood-Brain Barrier
Endogenous OXT does **not** freely cross the BBB at normal physiological concentrations. The central and peripheral OXT systems function largely **independently**. Intranasal OXT bypasses the BBB via olfactory/trigeminal nerve routes, reaching CNS compartments within 30–45 min.

### Receptors
**OXTR**: Single known receptor subtype. Gq-coupled GPCR. Some cross-reactivity with V1a/V1b vasopressin receptors at very high concentrations (shared structural homology).

**CNS expression**: Hypothalamus, medial amygdala, central amygdala, BNST, nucleus accumbens, ventral pallidum, PFC, hippocampus, olfactory bulb, dorsal vagal nucleus, locus coeruleus.

**Peripheral expression**: Uterus, mammary glands, kidney, heart, vascular smooth muscle, immune cells.

### Receptor Sensitivity and Desensitization
- OXTR undergoes homologous desensitization via GRK phosphorylation and β-arrestin recruitment.
- **During late pregnancy**: OXTR density in uterus upregulates dramatically (~80–200×) — a rare positive up-regulation before labor.
- **Chronic intranasal OXT**: can downregulate OXTR over time; tolerance evidence exists.
- **Social isolation**: increases OXTR expression (upregulation in the "hungry for bonding" state).

### CNS vs. Peripheral
**Both**. Acts as neurotransmitter/neuromodulator in CNS and as classical hormone in periphery.

### Degradation and Half-Life
| Compartment | Half-Life |
|---|---|
| Plasma (IV) | ~1–6 min; typically cited 3–5 min |
| CSF | 20+ min (less enzymatic activity) |

Degraded by plasma oxytocinases/aminopeptidases (LNPEP, which rises substantially late in pregnancy) and in liver/kidneys.

### Key Brain Regions
PVN/SON (source), medial/central amygdala (fear regulation), nucleus accumbens (reward bonding), BNST (stress modulation), PFC (social cognition), uterus, mammary glands, heart.

### Primary Effects
- Uterine contraction, milk letdown, bonding facilitation
- Increased social approach motivation, social recognition, facial emotion recognition
- Trust, generosity
- Anxiety modulation: context-dependent — anxiolytic vs. amygdala fear suppression for in-group; can amplify vigilance toward out-group
- Mild analgesia (via PAG OXTRs)
- Anti-inflammatory, cardioprotective, blood pressure regulation

### High OXT Phenotype
Warmth, love, trust, social ease, openness. Reduced fear response to threatening faces. Heightened sense of connection during touch, eye contact, and close bonding. "Cuddle hormone" phenomenology.

### Low OXT Phenotype
Social withdrawal, difficulty trusting, heightened amygdala reactivity, loneliness, reduced parental bonding impulse. Chronic low OXT is associated with autism spectrum features, social anxiety, borderline personality disorder.

## β-ENDORPHIN AND ENKEPHALINS (Endogenous Opioids)

### Class and Type
**β-Endorphin**: 31-amino acid neuropeptide derived from the POMC (pro-opiomelanocortin) precursor. The most potent endogenous opioid.

**Enkephalins (Met- and Leu-enkephalin)**: Pentapeptides (5 amino acids) from proenkephalin (PENK):
- Met-enkephalin: Tyr-Gly-Gly-Phe-Met
- Leu-enkephalin: Tyr-Gly-Gly-Phe-Leu

Also in the opioid family: **Dynorphins** (from prodynorphin/PDYN; kappa-preferring).

### Where Synthesized

**β-Endorphin**:
- CNS: **Arcuate nucleus** of the hypothalamus (POMC neurons) and nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) of the medulla.
- **Anterior and intermediate pituitary** (POMC-expressing corticotrophs): major source of circulating β-endorphin. POMC cleaved by prohormone convertases (PC1/3 in pituitary; PC2 in brain) to yield β-endorphin, ACTH, α-MSH.

**Enkephalins**:
- Widely distributed: striatum, spinal cord (dorsal horn), brainstem, limbic areas.
- **Adrenal medulla** (chromaffin cells): major source of circulating enkephalins.
- Gut enteric NS.

### Where Stored
- LDCVs, often co-packaged with other co-transmitters.
- β-Endorphin in Herring bodies of posterior pituitary; also in anterior pituitary secretory granules alongside ACTH.

### Release Dynamics
**β-Endorphin**:
- Co-released with ACTH from anterior pituitary in a **1:1 molar ratio** — both from the same POMC precursor. Every HPA stress response releases β-endorphin into blood alongside cortisol.
- Primarily **phasic, stress/pain-driven** in brain and pituitary.
- Low tonic baseline; marked phasic surges with pain, exercise, acute stress, laughter, bonding peaks.

**Enkephalins**:
- Released phasically in response to pain signals in spinal cord (gate-control modulation), acute stress, reward-seeking behaviors.
- Very short-lived due to rapid enzymatic degradation.

### Units of Measurement
- β-Endorphin (plasma): **pg/mL**
- Met-enkephalin (plasma/CSF): **pg/mL** or **pmol/mL**

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

**β-Endorphin**:
| Condition | Plasma Concentration |
|---|---|
| Resting baseline (healthy adults) | ~10–50 pg/mL; one large cohort: 43.7 ± 28 pg/mL |
| Fit individuals (resting) | ~153–188 pg/mL (fitness-matched cohorts) |
| Moderate exercise (~70% VO₂max, 25 min) | Rises from ~31 to ~95 pg/mL (3× increase) |
| High-intensity exercise / marathon | ~200–300 pg/mL |
| Labor and delivery | ~200–400 pg/mL (among highest physiological conditions) |
| CSF | 10–20 fmol/mL (volume transmission; diffuse) |

**Met-enkephalin**:
| Compartment | Concentration |
|---|---|
| Plasma | 14–140 pg/mL |
| CSF (lumbar) | 5–29 pg/mL; mean ~3.4 pmol/mL |

*Wide variation in β-endorphin values reflects assay methodology (cross-reactivity with ACTH fragments, extracted vs. unextracted) and subject fitness.*

### Receptors
All three are Gi/Go-coupled GPCRs. Activation inhibits adenylyl cyclase, reduces cAMP, decreases neuronal excitability.

| Receptor | Primary Ligand | Location | Key Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mu-opioid (MOR/OPRM1)** | β-Endorphin | PAG, limbic, VTA, NAc, dorsal horn, brainstem | Analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression, reward |
| **Delta-opioid (DOR/OPRD1)** | Enkephalins | Limbic, striatum, cortex | Mood elevation, antidepressant effects, analgesia |
| **Kappa-opioid (KOR/OPRK1)** | Dynorphins | Spinal cord, limbic | Analgesia but also **dysphoria**, sedation, stress-induced aversion |

### Receptor Sensitivity and Desensitization
- **MOR desensitization**: rapid phosphorylation by GRK2/3/5/6 → β-arrestin recruitment → receptor internalization.
- β-Endorphin (unlike morphine) **promotes receptor internalization**, which paradoxically helps reset/recycle receptors and may limit tolerance development compared to exogenous opioids.
- Chronic stimulation → receptor downregulation, reduced cAMP signaling, functional tolerance.
- MOR-DOR heterodimers can form and alter opioid pharmacology.

### CNS vs. Peripheral
**Both**. β-Endorphin found in CNS and blood. Enkephalins are CNS-central and in adrenal medulla/gut. Peripheral opioid receptors on sensory neurons, immune cells, and GI tissue — relevant for peripheral analgesia.

### Degradation and Half-Life
| Peptide | Half-Life | Degradation |
|---|---|---|
| β-Endorphin | ~10–30 min (plasma) | Enkephalinases (neprilysin, aminopeptidase N), non-specific peptidases |
| Enkephalins | **~1–2 min** (plasma/tissue) | Extremely rapid hydrolysis by enkephalinases (neprilysin/NEP, DPP3, aminopeptidase N, ACE) at Tyr-Gly bond |

Enkephalin's ultra-short half-life is why enkephalin analogs and NEP inhibitors are pharmacological targets.

### Key Brain Regions
PAG (central analgesic gateway), arcuate nucleus, anterior pituitary, VTA, nucleus accumbens, limbic system, dorsal horn of spinal cord.

### Primary Effects
- Analgesia (MOR in PAG, dorsal horn, thalamus)
- Euphoria/wellbeing (MOR in VTA/NAc, disinhibits dopamine)
- Respiratory depression (MOR in brainstem; clinically critical in opioid overdose)
- Stress buffering (reduces subjective distress response to pain/acute stress)
- Immune modulation (opioid receptors on immune cells)
- Gut motility reduction
- Social bonding "warmth" — β-endorphin specifically implicated in pain-tolerance increase from sustained laughter and music listening

**Note on runner's high**: A 2021 study (Siebers et al., Psychoneuroendocrinology) found that blocking opioid receptors with naltrexone did not prevent exercise-induced euphoria/anxiolysis, implicating endocannabinoids rather than endorphins as the primary mediator of the euphoric affect. Endorphins remain important for exercise-induced **analgesia** and **social bonding**.

### High β-Endorphin/Enkephalin Phenotype
Profound wellbeing, warmth, euphoria, pain relief, emotional buffering. The "warm glow" of exercise, laughter, prosocial bonding. Deep sense of safety.

### Low Endogenous Opioid Tone Phenotype
Social pain sensitivity, increased ache of loneliness, heightened grief, blunted pleasure (anhedonia). Chronic low tone associated with social isolation, depression, possibly fibromyalgia-type pain amplification.

## CORTISOL

### Class and Type
**Glucocorticoid steroid hormone** (C₂₁H₃₀O₅, MW 362 Da). Synthesized from cholesterol via steroidogenesis. Primary endogenous glucocorticoid in humans. Classified as both a stress hormone and a metabolic regulator.

### Where Synthesized
**Zona fasciculata** of the **adrenal cortex** (bilateral adrenal glands). Cortisol synthesis is induced by ACTH binding to MC2R receptors on adrenocortical cells, activating StAR protein and CYP enzymes (CYP11A1, CYP17A1, CYP21A2, CYP11B1) in mitochondria and ER.

**HPA Axis control**:
- **CRH** (hypothalamic PVN) → **ACTH** (anterior pituitary corticotrophs) → **Cortisol** (adrenal cortex)

### Where Stored
- **Not stored** in secretory granules. As a lipophilic steroid, synthesized on-demand and diffuses immediately out of the cell.
- In blood: ~90–95% protein-bound (~70% to corticosteroid-binding globulin CBG; ~20% to albumin). Only **~5–10% is free cortisol** (biologically active fraction).

### Release Dynamics

**Circadian rhythm (tonic with rhythmic variation)**:
| Time | Serum Cortisol | Salivary Cortisol |
|---|---|---|
| ~Midnight–2:00 AM (nadir) | <50 nmol/L | ~1–2 nmol/L |
| ~2:00–3:00 AM (rise begins) | Rising | Rising |
| Wake | ~180–300 nmol/L | ~15 nmol/L |
| **Cortisol Awakening Response (CAR)** +30 min | ~50–100% above wake | ~23–35 nmol/L |
| Morning peak 8:00–9:00 AM | **300–600 nmol/L** | — |
| 4:00 PM | 85–275 nmol/L | — |
| Late night (23:00) | <140 nmol/L | <4 nmol/L |

Pulsatile secretion ~15–18 ultradian pulses per day (~100 nmol/L amplitude each). CAR is a distinct additional spike 30–45 min after waking, driven by anticipatory and circadian processes; a sensitive HPA axis marker.

**Acute stress (phasic)**:
- Rise within 15–20 min of stressor onset.
- Peak at 20–30 min post-stressor.
- Returns to baseline within 60–90 min for acute stressors.
- Major surgery: can reach 800–1000+ nmol/L.
- ITT gold standard: normal response > 500 nmol/L.

### Units of Measurement
- **Serum/plasma**: nmol/L (SI) or µg/dL. Conversion: 1 µg/dL = 27.6 nmol/L.
- **Saliva**: nmol/L or ng/mL (reflects free fraction only).
- **Urine (24h)**: nmol/day or µg/day (urinary free cortisol, UFC).

### Receptors
Both are nuclear receptors (ligand-activated transcription factors). Cortisol crosses the cell membrane, binds in cytosol, then translocates to nucleus and binds glucocorticoid response elements (GREs).

| Receptor | Affinity (Kd) | Occupied At | Location | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mineralocorticoid receptor (MR/NR3C2)** | ~0.5–1 nM (high) | Basal cortisol levels | Hippocampus, amygdala, cardiovascular cells, kidney | Basal HPA tone, memory consolidation, mood stability |
| **Glucocorticoid receptor (GR/NR3C1)** | ~5–30 nM (lower) | Stress peaks | Ubiquitous — brain, liver, immune cells, muscle, bone | Metabolic effects, immune suppression, negative feedback |

### HPA Negative Feedback Loop
1. Cortisol binds GR/MR in hypothalamic PVN → suppresses CRH gene expression.
2. Cortisol binds GR in anterior pituitary → suppresses ACTH release.
3. **Fast feedback** (nongenomic, seconds–minutes): membrane-mediated GR activation; endocannabinoid-mediated suppression of CRH synaptic input.
4. **Slow feedback** (genomic, hours): downregulation of CRH/ACTH gene transcription.
5. **Chronic stress** → GR downregulation in hippocampus and PFC (reducing feedback sensitivity) → HPA less braked → elevated basal cortisol. Self-perpetuating cycle.

### Receptor Sensitivity and Habituation
- GR downregulation under chronic hypercortisolemia: fewer receptors per cell, reduced transcriptional sensitivity.
- MR occupancy at baseline supports hippocampal neurogenesis; GR over-activation (chronic stress) suppresses neurogenesis and causes hippocampal atrophy (CA3 dendritic retraction).
- PTSD paradox: often shows **low** cortisol despite hyperarousal — due to receptor **hypersensitization** (GR density increases, feedback too sensitive; different from classic hypercortisolemic depression).

### CNS vs. Peripheral
**Both**. Produced peripherally; crosses BBB readily (lipophilic steroid); profound CNS effects via GR/MR.

### Degradation and Half-Life
| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Plasma half-life | ~60–90 min (range 40–130 min) |
| Biological half-life (GRE-driven gene effects) | 8–12 hours |

Metabolized by 11β-HSD2 (cortisol → cortisone, inactive) and liver reduction/conjugation → excreted as glucuronide/sulfate conjugates.

### Key Brain Regions
**Hippocampus** (dense MR/GR — most vulnerable to cortisol-mediated damage), **amygdala** (fear memory consolidation), **PFC** (executive function; GR-mediated), **hypothalamus** (HPA feedback).

### Primary Effects

**Acute (moderate cortisol)**:
- Mobilizes glucose (gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis), free fatty acids, amino acids
- Suppresses immune/inflammatory response
- Enhances memory consolidation
- Increases alertness and arousal
- Cardiovascular: increases blood pressure, cardiac output

**Chronic/excess**:
- Hippocampal atrophy (dendritic retraction, impaired neurogenesis)
- PFC thinning
- Amygdala sensitization
- Impaired explicit memory
- Weight gain (central adiposity), hyperglycemia, immune suppression, osteoporosis, sleep disruption

**Insufficient**:
- Addison's phenotype: fatigue, hypoglycemia, hypotension, poor stress resilience, salt cravings

### High Cortisol Phenotype
**Acute**: Heightened focus, urgency, clarity ("eustress"). Short-term fear/emotional memory enhancement.
**Chronic**: Anxiety, irritability, emotional dysregulation, poor working memory, paranoia, sleep fragmentation. Signature of burnout and PTSD-hyperarousal variants.

### Low Cortisol Phenotype
Extreme fatigue, inability to handle any stress, depressive low energy, emotional flatness, brain fog. Seen in Addison's disease and burnout-associated hypocortisolism.

## GHRELIN

### Class and Type
28-amino acid **peptide hormone** and orexigenic neuropeptide. The primary endogenous ligand for the growth hormone secretagogue receptor (GHSR-1a). The **only known circulating orexigenic (appetite-stimulating) hormone**.

**Two principal forms**:
- **Acyl-ghrelin (AG)**: Active form, octanoylated at Ser-3 by ghrelin O-acyltransferase (GOAT). Binds GHSR-1a.
- **Desacyl-ghrelin (DAG)**: Inactive form (~60–80% of total circulating ghrelin). Does not bind GHSR-1a but has independent biological roles via an unidentified receptor.

### Where Synthesized
- Primarily: **X/A-like cells (P/D1 cells in humans)** of the **oxyntic glands in the gastric fundus and corpus**. ~20% of gastric endocrine cells.
- Secondary: small intestine, pancreatic ε-cells, pituitary, hypothalamic arcuate nucleus neurons, kidney, placenta.
- Synthesis: Prepro-ghrelin (117 aa) → pro-ghrelin → ghrelin + obestatin. GOAT enzyme adds octanoyl (8-carbon) fatty acid to Ser-3.

### Where Stored
Stored in secretory granules within X/A-like cells. After secretion, circulates primarily in plasma.

### Release Dynamics
- **Pulsatile/preprandial pattern**: rises sharply before anticipated meals (15–30 min prior) and falls within 30–60 min after eating — even before nutrients are absorbed. Cephalic phase regulation via vagal and enteric NS signals.
- The preprandial spike is considered the key signal for meal *initiation*.
- Circadian: lowest ~noon, highest pre-dinner/late evening (standard meal-pattern humans).
- **Fasting**: Total ghrelin rises progressively, but acyl-ghrelin fraction may not rise proportionally (GOAT substrate — octanoate from dietary MCFAs — is depleted with very prolonged fasting).
- **Stress**: Acute psychological and physical stress trigger ghrelin release independently of meal patterns.
- **Sleep**: Ghrelin rises during sleep, contributing to nocturnal GH pulsatility.

### Units of Measurement
- Plasma: **pg/mL**
- CSF: detectable in pg/mL (rarely measured)

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

| Condition | Total Ghrelin | Acyl-Ghrelin | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fasting, healthy adults | 300–700 pg/mL (typical ~520–700 pg/mL) | ~78–100 pg/mL (~10–20% of total) | Obese individuals have **lower** ghrelin (insulin resistance suppresses it paradoxically) |
| Desacyl-ghrelin (fasting) | — | ~85–175 pg/mL | Measured independently |
| Preprandial peak | 700–900+ pg/mL | ~+10–20 pg/mL above fasting | ~2–3× postprandial nadir |
| Postprandial nadir | ~300–400 pg/mL | Falls within 60 min of meal | — |
| Post-gastric bypass | Dramatically reduced | — | Fundus largely bypassed; contributes to reduced appetite |

### Receptors

**GHSR-1a** (Growth Hormone Secretagogue Receptor 1a): Gq/G11 and Gi-coupled GPCR. **Uniquely high constitutive (basal/ligand-independent) activity** — ~50% of maximum signaling even without ligand. This baseline activity is itself physiologically important for dopamine signaling and mood.

| Location (CNS) | Function |
|---|---|
| Hypothalamic arcuate and VMN | Food intake control |
| VTA (dopamine neurons) | Reward, motivation, mood |
| Nucleus accumbens | Food reward, hedonic eating |
| Hippocampus | Memory, neurogenesis |
| Amygdala | Anxiety, mood |
| Pituitary | GH release |

**GHSR-1b**: Truncated splice variant. Does not bind ghrelin; can heterodimerize with GHSR-1a and attenuate signaling.

### Receptor Sensitivity and Desensitization
- GHSR-1a: rapid homologous desensitization after sustained activation.
- **Constitutive activity**: even without ghrelin, GHSR-1a expressed in brain provides tonic dopamine signaling.
- Fasting upregulates GHSR-1a in some brain regions → increased sensitivity when ghrelin is highest (reinforcing hunger).
- Obesity/high-fat diet: desensitizes reward circuitry; contributes to blunted reward and compensatory overeating.

### CNS vs. Peripheral
**Both**. Primarily peripheral hormone produced by the gut. Crosses BBB and enters brain via circumventricular organs. Central ghrelin-producing neurons also exist in the hypothalamus.

### Degradation and Half-Life
| Form | Half-Life | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Acyl-ghrelin | ~9–13 min | Plasma esterases/butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) → deacylation to desacyl form |
| Total ghrelin (desacyl-dominant) | ~27–31 min | Neprilysin, DPP-IV, and other peptidases |

### Key Brain Regions
Arcuate nucleus (appetite), VTA (reward/dopamine), nucleus accumbens (motivation), hippocampus (memory/neurogenesis), amygdala (anxiety/mood), pituitary (GH release).

### Primary Effects
- **Hunger/appetite**: activates arcuate NPY/AgRP neurons → increased food intake and fat storage
- **GH release**: major physiological GH secretagogue (especially during sleep)
- **Metabolic**: promotes fat storage, reduces fat oxidation, increases adipogenesis
- **Reward/dopamine**: direct activation of VTA dopamine neurons → increased food reward motivation
- **Mood (antidepressant-like)**: enhances monoamine levels; increases BDNF and CREB phosphorylation; promotes neurogenesis
- **Anxiolytic**: GHSR-1a activation in amygdala reduces anxiety-like behavior
- **Gastric motility**: increases gastric acid secretion and motility
- **Cardiovascular**: vasodilatory, cardioprotective

### High Ghrelin Phenotype
Powerful hunger urge, food-seeking motivation, heightened reward salience for food (and other pleasures — ghrelin amplifies dopamine response to non-food rewards too). Paradoxically, elevated ghrelin buffers against stress-induced depression — animals with chronically elevated ghrelin show less depression-like behavior after chronic stress. Emotionally: hunger + heightened motivation + restlessness.

### Low Ghrelin Phenotype
Satiety, reduced motivation, contentment. In obesity, chronically suppressed ghrelin contributes to blunted reward circuitry — reward insensitivity drives compensatory overeating.

## OREXIN / HYPOCRETIN (Orexin-A and Orexin-B)

### Class and Type
Neuropeptides derived from the same precursor, prepro-orexin (130 aa). Function as **CNS neuromodulators/neurotransmitters** with very limited peripheral presence.

| Peptide | Size | Structure | Receptor Affinity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Orexin-A (Hypocretin-1)** | 33 aa | N-terminal pyroglutamyl; two intrachain disulfide bonds; structurally stable | High affinity at both OX1R and OX2R |
| **Orexin-B (Hypocretin-2)** | 28 aa | Linear peptide; no disulfide bonds; less stable | Preferentially binds OX2R |

### Where Synthesized
- Exclusively in the **lateral hypothalamic area (LHA)** and adjacent **perifornical area (PFA)** and **dorsomedial hypothalamus (DMH)**.
- Human brain: **~50,000–80,000 orexin-producing neurons total** — an astonishingly small population exerting broad regulatory control.
- No significant peripheral production (trace in adrenal gland, gut — physiologically minor).

### Where Stored
- LDCVs in axon terminals and cell bodies.
- Dendrodendritic and axonal release both occur.
- CSF contains detectable orexin-A from extracellular diffusion.

### Release Dynamics
- **State-dependent tonic regulation**: orexin neurons fire most strongly during **active wakefulness** (especially high motor activity, emotional arousal, positive reward-seeking). Fire decreases during quiet waking; near-silent during NREM; **completely silent during REM**.
- Orexin is a **wakefulness-stabilizing** system — prevents transitions from wakefulness into sleep, and most critically prevents REM motor inhibition from intruding into waking (failure = cataplexy).
- **Emotional modulation**: peaks during positive arousal, goal-directed behavior, anger, social interaction.
- **Food/reward seeking**: specific LH orexin neurons are activated by food cues, drugs of abuse, and conditioned reward stimuli — independent of the arousal function.

### Units of Measurement
- CSF: **pg/mL**
- Plasma: technically <5–10 pg/mL (mostly below detection with standard assays; not a reliable clinical measurement)

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

| Condition | CSF Orexin-A | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Healthy subjects (normal) | **250–500 pg/mL** (broader range: 200–600 pg/mL) | Clinical threshold: normal if >200 pg/mL |
| Narcolepsy Type 1 (with cataplexy) | **≤110 pg/mL** (most undetectable, <50 pg/mL) | 85–95% of orexin neurons destroyed by autoimmune process |
| Intermediate hypersomnia zone | 111–200 pg/mL | Associated with some hypersomnia disorders |
| Plasma | <5–10 pg/mL | Most ultrasensitive assays: ~10–50 pg/mL range; not well validated |

CSF orexin-A does not show sharp meal-entrained or stress-entrained spikes (unlike ghrelin/cortisol). It reflects integrated tonic release.

### Receptors

| Receptor | G-Protein | Affinity | Expression (High Density) | Primary Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **OX1R** (Orexin Receptor 1) | Gq | High for orexin-A (~10× > orexin-B) | Locus coeruleus (highest), PFC, hippocampus, VTA, dorsal raphe, amygdala, BNST | Arousal, reward, attention/cognition, stress response |
| **OX2R** (Orexin Receptor 2) | Gi and Gq | High for both (non-selective) | Tuberomammillary nucleus (histamine, very high), cerebral cortex, NAc, anterior hypothalamus, hippocampus, raphe | **Wake promotion** (most critical for sleep/wake switching); REM suppression (OX2R agonism more effective for wakefulness than OX1R) |

Both signal via Gq (PLC → IP3 → DAG → PKC, Ca²⁺) and also via G12/13 and Gs in some cell types.

### Receptor Sensitivity and Desensitization
- Orexin receptors desensitize with sustained agonist exposure (GRK/β-arrestin pathway).
- In NT1 narcolepsy: massive receptor upregulation after orexin neuron loss → remaining receptors sensitized; exploited by OX2R agonist therapies (danavorexton).
- Chronic sleep deprivation: altered orexin receptor sensitivity in animal models.

### CNS vs. Peripheral
**Predominantly CNS**. BBB limits peripheral peptide access to brain orexin receptors. Trace peripheral expression (gut, adrenal, testes) is physiologically minor.

### Degradation and Half-Life
| Peptide | Half-Life | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Orexin-A | ~27 min (in vivo) | N-terminal pyroglutamyl modification and disulfide bonds confer stability |
| Orexin-B | Shorter (linear peptide; less stable) | More rapidly degraded by plasma peptidases |

Degraded by non-specific endopeptidases (neprilysin, aminopeptidases). CSF orexin-A is relatively stable, consistent with tonic neuromodulatory function.

### Key Brain Regions
Lateral hypothalamus (source), locus coeruleus (NE arousal), tuberomammillary nucleus (histamine arousal), dorsal raphe (serotonin), VTA (dopamine/reward), nucleus accumbens, PFC, amygdala.

### Primary Effects
- **Wakefulness maintenance**: tonic excitation of LC (NE), dorsal raphe (5-HT), tuberomammillary nucleus (histamine), LDT/PPT (ACh) — "locks" brain in wakefulness via redundant drive to all major arousal systems
- **REM sleep suppression**: inhibits sublaterodorsal nucleus; prevents REM atonia intrusion into waking
- **Appetite**: orexigenic via arcuate NPY pathways; stimulates locomotion for food-seeking
- **Reward and motivation**: LH orexin → VTA dopamine → enhanced dopamine for reward cue responses; critical for cue-conditioned reward-seeking and addiction vulnerability
- **Autonomic regulation**: increases heart rate, blood pressure; coordinates sympathetic arousal
- **HPA axis**: activates CRH neurons; stress increases orexin release, amplifying cortisol output
- **Cognition**: enhances attention, working memory, executive function via NE and 5-HT input to cortex

### High Orexin Phenotype
Bright alertness, energy, motivation, heightened curiosity and goal-directedness. The "locked-in" engaged state — difficult to wind down. Associated with positive emotional valence during social interaction and active behavior. Anger and frustration also reliably increase orexin firing.

### Low Orexin / Orexin Deficiency (Narcolepsy Type 1)
Profound excessive daytime sleepiness, irresistible sleep attacks, **cataplexy** (emotional triggers — laughter, surprise, anger — cause sudden muscle paralysis; orexin system fails to suppress REM motor circuits during waking), hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations, sleep paralysis. Beyond sleep: blunted motivation, reduced reward sensitivity, metabolic dysregulation (weight gain despite unchanged calories), emotional dysregulation.

## Cross-Chemical Interactions

| Axis | Interaction |
|---|---|
| Ghrelin → Orexin | Ghrelin activates orexin neurons in LHA; both converge on food-seeking arousal |
| Orexin → HPA (Cortisol) | Orexin activates CRH neurons → amplifies cortisol stress response |
| Cortisol ↔ β-Endorphin | ACTH and β-endorphin co-released (POMC collar); cortisol feedback eventually dampens further opioid/ACTH release |
| β-Endorphin → Ghrelin | Opioid receptor activation in gut/hypothalamus can modulate ghrelin release patterns |
| Oxytocin → β-Endorphin | Social bonding behaviors co-activate both systems; OXT may prime opioid release in limbic circuits |
| Orexin → Dopamine | OX1R/OX2R on VTA dopamine neurons; orexin increases dopamine firing for reward/motivation |
| Ghrelin → Dopamine | GHSR-1a directly excites VTA neurons; ghrelin's orexigenic effect is partly dopaminergic |
| Cortisol → Orexin | Glucocorticoids modulate orexin expression; reciprocal stress-arousal coupling |
