# Amino Acids and Other Signaling Molecules

Glutamate, GABA, Adenosine, Acetylcholine, Anandamide, Substance P, NPY, CRH.

---

## GLUTAMATE

### Class and Type
Amino acid neurotransmitter; the **principal excitatory neurotransmitter** in the vertebrate CNS. Both a metabolic amino acid and a fast synaptic signaling molecule.

### Where Synthesized
Two primary pathways:
1. **Glutamine pathway**: Glutamine taken up from astrocytes → converted to glutamate by mitochondrial phosphate-activated glutaminase (PAG). This is the **glutamate-glutamine cycle**: astrocytes take up released glutamate → convert it to glutamine via glutamine synthetase → release glutamine back to neurons.
2. **TCA cycle**: Alpha-ketoglutarate from the citric acid cycle is transaminated to glutamate.

### Where Stored
- Packaged into synaptic vesicles via **vesicular glutamate transporters (VGLUT1, VGLUT2, VGLUT3)**.
- Vesicular concentration: ~60–210 mM.
- Intracellular (cytoplasmic) glutamate in neurons: ~10 mM.

### Release Pattern
- **Phasic**: Action-potential-triggered Ca²⁺-dependent exocytosis → brief, massive glutamate release into synaptic cleft.
- **Tonic**: Low-level non-vesicular release from reversed transporters and glial release contributes to ambient extracellular glutamate.

### Units of Measurement
| Context | Unit |
|---|---|
| Intracellular / vesicular | mM |
| Extracellular tonic/ambient | µM |
| Between-event extrasynaptic floor | nM |
| CSF, plasma | µM |

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

| Compartment | Concentration |
|---|---|
| Intracellular (neurons) | ~10 mM |
| Synaptic vesicles | 60–210 mM |
| Synaptic cleft at peak release | **>1 mM** (for <10 ms) |
| Extracellular (ambient, microdialysis) | 1–5 µM (range 0.02–20 µM depending on method) |
| Extrasynaptic floor (between-event) | <20 nM |
| CSF (human) | ~10 µM (range 5–15 µM) |
| Plasma | ~150 µM |

### Receptors

**Ionotropic (ligand-gated ion channels — fast)**:
| Receptor | Ions | Distribution | Key Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AMPA (GluA1–4)** | Na⁺/K⁺ | Virtually all excitatory synapses; cortex, hippocampus, cerebellum, striatum | Fast EPSPs; initial depolarization that unlocks NMDA |
| **NMDA (GluN1 + GluN2A–D + GluN3)** | Ca²⁺, Na⁺, K⁺ | Ubiquitous; highest in hippocampus, PFC, amygdala | Coincidence detector (requires glycine/D-serine co-agonist AND voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ unblocking); primary gate for synaptic plasticity |
| **Kainate (GluK1–5)** | Na⁺/K⁺ | Hippocampus (high), cerebellum, spinal cord | Pre- and postsynaptic roles; modulates both excitatory and inhibitory transmission |

**Metabotropic (G-protein coupled — slow/modulatory)**:
| Group | Receptors | Coupling | Location | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Group I** | mGluR1, mGluR5 | Gq | Postsynaptic/perisynaptic; PFC, hippocampus, striatum, cerebellum | Enhance excitability; synaptic plasticity and LTP |
| **Group II** | mGluR2, mGluR3 | Gi/Go | Presynaptic autoreceptors/heteroreceptors; cortex, limbic | Inhibit adenylyl cyclase → reduce glutamate release |
| **Group III** | mGluR4, 6, 7, 8 | Gi/Go | Presynaptic; basal ganglia, cerebellum | Inhibitory; mGluR7 = lowest affinity ("overflow guard"); acts only at very high glutamate |

### Receptor Sensitivity, LTP/LTD

**LTP (Long-Term Potentiation)**:
NMDA receptor Ca²⁺ influx → CaMKII activation → AMPA receptor insertion into postsynaptic membrane (exocytosis + lateral diffusion from extrasynaptic sites) → increased synaptic strength. Substrate of learning and memory.

**LTD (Long-Term Depression)**:
Sustained low-frequency stimulation or mGluR1/5 activation → AMPA receptor endocytosis → weakened synapse. Substrate of forgetting and synaptic pruning.

Repeated NMDA activation → receptor downregulation (desensitization) over hours. Group II/III mGluR autoreceptors limit excessive release via feedback inhibition.

### CNS vs. Peripheral
Primarily **CNS**. Also in peripheral sensory neurons, enteric NS, and as a metabolic amino acid in plasma. Neurotransmitter role is almost exclusively central.

### Reuptake and Degradation
**Reuptake dominates**. High-affinity excitatory amino acid transporters (EAATs):
- **EAAT2 (GLT-1)**: Astrocytic; responsible for **>90% of CNS glutamate clearance**.
- EAAT1, EAAT3, EAAT4, EAAT5: additional glial/neuronal contribution.
- Synaptic cleft glutamate clears within **~1 ms**.
- In astrocytes: glutamate → glutamine by glutamine synthetase → recycled.
- No significant extracellular enzymatic degradation.

**Effective synaptic half-life**: milliseconds.

### Key Brain Regions
Cortex (all layers), hippocampus (CA1–CA3, dentate gyrus), amygdala, striatum, cerebellum, thalamus, brainstem.

### Primary Effects
- Excitatory drive throughout CNS
- Learning and memory formation (NMDA-dependent LTP in hippocampus)
- Working memory (PFC)
- Sensory processing, motor coordination
- **Excitotoxicity** at pathological levels: Ca²⁺ overload → oxidative stress → mitochondrial failure → cell death

### High Glutamate Phenotype
Hyperarousal, racing thoughts, anxiety, muscle tension; seizure activity in extremis. Chronic overactivation: progressive neurotoxicity, cognitive decline. Acutely (ketamine paradox): NMDA block at subanesthetic doses produces dissociation and rapid antidepressant effects.

### Low Functional Glutamate Phenotype
Cognitive slowing, difficulty forming memories, sedation. At extreme NMDA blockade: dissociation, hallucinogen-like states, analgesia.

## GABA (Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid)

### Class and Type
Amino acid neurotransmitter; the **principal inhibitory neurotransmitter** of the CNS. Produced from glutamate by decarboxylation.

### Where Synthesized
From glutamate by **glutamate decarboxylase (GAD)**, two isoforms:
- **GAD65**: synaptic, release-associated
- **GAD67**: cytoplasmic, tonic production

Both require pyridoxal-5-phosphate (vitamin B6) as cofactor.

GABAergic interneurons are found throughout the brain. Highest density: cortex, hippocampus, cerebellum (Purkinje cells), basal ganglia, thalamus.

### Where Stored
Packaged into synaptic vesicles via **VIAAT / VGAT** (vesicular inhibitory amino acid transporter).

### Release Pattern
- **Phasic**: Ca²⁺-dependent exocytosis from fast-spiking interneurons (e.g., parvalbumin-positive basket cells) → brief high-concentration bursts.
- **Tonic**: Ambient extracellular GABA at low µM continuously activates high-affinity extrasynaptic GABA-A receptors → persistent inhibitory tone regulating overall excitability.

### Units of Measurement
| Context | Unit |
|---|---|
| Synaptic cleft at peak | mM |
| Extracellular ambient | µM |
| CSF | pmol/mL |
| Plasma | nM to low µM |

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

| Compartment | Concentration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Intracellular (GABAergic neurons) | ~5–10 mM | — |
| Synaptic cleft at peak | 1–5 mM (some sources up to 10 mM); duration <1 ms | — |
| Extracellular (ambient/tonic) | 0.1–1 µM (submicromolar to low µM) | — |
| CSF (human, free GABA) | 127–239 pmol/mL (0.1–0.25 µM) by standard assay; one large study median ~4.11 µM | Methodological variation is significant; free vs. conjugated GABA differs enormously (conjugated ~2885 pmol/mL vs. free ~65 pmol/mL) |
| Plasma | Low nM range | Variable |

### Receptors

**GABA-A (ionotropic, ligand-gated Cl⁻ channel)**:
- Heteropentamers (α, β, γ, δ, ε, etc. subunits — 19 known). Most common: α1β2γ2.
- Hyperpolarization via Cl⁻ influx.

| Context | Subunit Type | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Synaptic (phasic)** | γ-containing (e.g., α1β2γ2) | Responds to brief mM GABA pulses; fast inhibition |
| **Extrasynaptic (tonic)** | δ-containing (e.g., α4β3δ) | High-affinity; persistent activation by low ambient GABA; tonic inhibition |

**Subunit pharmacology**:
- α1: sedation
- α2/3: anxiolysis
- α5 (hippocampal): cognitive effects

**GABA-B (metabotropic, Gi-coupled GPCR)**:
- Heterodimer of GABA-B1 and GABA-B2.
- **Presynaptic**: suppresses neurotransmitter release (GABA and glutamate) via reduced Ca²⁺ entry.
- **Postsynaptic**: opens GIRK (K⁺ inward rectifying) channels → slower, longer-lasting hyperpolarization.
- Cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, cerebellum, striatum, brainstem, peripheral ANS.

**Benzodiazepine mechanism**: Positive allosteric modulators at GABA-A; bind at α/γ subunit interface → increase channel open **frequency** (not duration or amplitude). Require GABA to be present. Result: enhanced Cl⁻ flux, global inhibitory tone increase.

**Alcohol mechanism**: Acts at GABA-A (especially δ-containing extrasynaptic receptors); also inhibits NMDA receptors. Combined: sedation + anxiolysis.

### Receptor Sensitivity and Habituation
- **Chronic benzodiazepine**: GABA-A receptor downregulation, subunit composition changes (γ2 and α1 redistribution), reduced Cl⁻ flux → tolerance. Abrupt withdrawal → rebound hyperexcitability (seizures, delirium tremens).
- **Tonic (δ-subunit) receptors**: less prone to rapid desensitization than synaptic receptors.
- **Chronic alcohol**: similar GABA-A subsensitivity; withdrawal → glutamate/NMDA hyperexcitability rebound.

### CNS vs. Peripheral
Primarily **CNS**. GABA-B receptors also in peripheral ANS.

### Reuptake and Degradation
**Reuptake** (primary clearance) via GABA transporters (GATs):
- **GAT-1**: predominant; presynaptic neurons and astrocytes.
- GAT-2, GAT-3: glial/neuronal.
- BGT-1 (GABA transporter 4): low affinity, extrasynaptic.

**Degradation**: GABA-transaminase (GABA-T) converts GABA to succinic semialdehyde → succinate (enters TCA cycle). Occurs mainly in astrocytes and mitochondria. GABA-T is the target of vigabatrin (antiepileptic).

~80% of GABA is recycled via reuptake; only ~20% lost per cycle.

**Effective synaptic half-life**: milliseconds (phasic) to seconds (tonic spillover).

### Key Brain Regions
Cortex (interneuron networks), hippocampus (basket/chandelier cells), basal ganglia (striatum, globus pallidus — major GABA output nuclei), thalamus, cerebellum (Purkinje cells → deep cerebellar nuclei), amygdala, hypothalamus, spinal cord dorsal horn.

### Primary Effects
- Global neural inhibition
- Anxiolysis, sedation, anticonvulsant
- Sleep facilitation (NREM)
- Muscle relaxation
- Antinociception at spinal level

### High GABA Phenotype
Calm, relaxed, reduced anxiety, sedation, mild cognitive slowing. Pharmacologically: benzodiazepine-like calm; at high levels — anesthesia.

### Low GABA Phenotype
Anxiety, hypervigilance, muscle tension, seizure susceptibility, insomnia, panic. CSF GABA is reduced in first-episode psychosis. Low GABA associated with depression, anxiety disorders, epilepsy, benzodiazepine/alcohol withdrawal.

## ADENOSINE

### Class and Type
**Purine nucleoside** (not a classical amino acid neurotransmitter). Acts as a neuromodulator and as the molecular accumulator of **homeostatic sleep pressure** (Process S).

### Where Synthesized / Produced
Not synthesized via exocytotic-vesicle pathway. Produced from:
1. **ATP catabolism**: Released ATP degraded extracellularly by ecto-nucleotidases (CD73, CD39) → AMP → adenosine.
2. **S-adenosyl homocysteine (SAH) hydrolysis**: Intracellular adenosine released when metabolic demand is high.
3. **cAMP hydrolysis** via 5'-nucleotidase.

Adenosine production is inherently tied to **neuronal metabolic activity** — more neural activity = more ATP turnover = more adenosine accumulation. A direct index of prior neural workload.

### Where Stored
Not stored in vesicles. Produced on-demand from extracellular ATP catabolism or released by equilibrative nucleoside transporters (ENTs) from intracellular pools.

### Release Pattern
**Tonic, continuously accumulating**: Adenosine builds steadily throughout wakefulness in proportion to neuronal activity and metabolic demand. Not phasic. Most pronounced in the **basal forebrain** and cortex. During NREM sleep, adenosine levels fall as the brain clears the accumulated load.

### Units of Measurement
- **nM (nanomolar)** — extracellular brain tissue (microdialysis), plasma, CSF

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

| Compartment | Concentration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Extracellular brain (microdialysis, awake) | ~30 nM (rodent) | Range across studies: 0.8–2100 nM (wide variation by technique) |
| Basal forebrain after 6 h sleep deprivation | ~140% of baseline (~42 nM estimated) | Progressive accumulation |
| CSF (human) | 40–90 nM | — |
| Plasma (human, normoxia) | ~12–17 nM | Radial artery ~12 nM; cubital vein ~17 nM |

**Note**: Caffeine does NOT reduce adenosine. It blocks adenosine receptors, allowing adenosine to continue accumulating. The "caffeine crash" occurs when caffeine clears and the accumulated adenosine suddenly gains receptor access.

### Receptors

| Receptor | Coupling | Distribution | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A1** | Gi-coupled | Cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, **basal forebrain** (highest), lateral hypothalamus, cerebellum | A1 activation in basal forebrain is the **primary driver of homeostatic sleep pressure**; inhibits wake-promoting neurons (cholinergic BF neurons, orexin neurons); reduces cAMP, opens GIRK channels, inhibits Ca²⁺ channels |
| **A2A** | Gs-coupled | **Striatum** (highest density in brain), olfactory tubercle, NAc, ventral striatum | Sleep onset (NAc/ventral striatum); modulates dopamine D2 signaling (A2A-D2 heterodimers); stimulates adenylyl cyclase |
| A2B | Gs-coupled (low affinity) | Peripheral; activated only at high adenosine | Inflammation, peripheral vasodilation |
| A3 | Low CNS expression | Primarily peripheral immune cells | Immune function |

### Receptor Sensitivity and Habituation
- **Chronic caffeine use** → upregulation of adenosine receptors → tolerance and rebound sleepiness/headache on cessation.
- After sleep deprivation: A1 receptor availability **increases** (upregulation, PET confirmed PNAS 2017); returns to baseline after recovery sleep.

### CNS vs. Peripheral
**Both**. CNS: homeostatic sleep pressure, attention, arousal. Peripheral: vasodilator (clinical IV adenosine for SVT), cardiac rhythm modulator, immune modulator.

### Degradation and Half-Life
- **ENT1/ENT2** (equilibrative nucleoside transporters, bidirectional): primary cellular uptake and clearance.
- **Adenosine deaminase (ADA)**: converts adenosine → inosine; major degradation enzyme.
- **Adenosine kinase**: phosphorylates adenosine → AMP.
- **Plasma half-life**: ~0.6–1.5 seconds in whole blood (extremely short); longer in tissue due to equilibrium buffering.

### Key Brain Regions
Basal forebrain (most critical for sleep pressure), cortex, hippocampus (A1), striatum (A2A), thalamus, lateral hypothalamus (A1 on orexin neurons).

### Primary Effects
- Sleepiness proportional to accumulated wakefulness
- Suppression of arousal-promoting neurons (basal forebrain cholinergic neurons via A1; orexin neurons via A1)
- Reduced cognitive sharpness, slower reaction times
- Decreased motivation, attentional fatigue
- Vasodilation and cardiac rate reduction (peripheral)

### High Adenosine Phenotype
Cognitive fatigue, difficulty concentrating, slowed reaction time, increased errors, emotional dysregulation, microsleeps, compulsive yawning, felt "heaviness." 16+ hours of wakefulness nearly doubles basal forebrain adenosine.

### Low Adenosine Phenotype
Alertness, clear thinking, quick reactions, motivational readiness (the state of early morning after full sleep).

## ACETYLCHOLINE (ACh)

### Class and Type
**Ester neurotransmitter** (choline + acetyl group). Functions as both a CNS neuromodulator and a peripheral neuromuscular/autonomic transmitter.

### Where Synthesized
By **choline acetyltransferase (ChAT)** at cholinergic nerve terminals: acetyl-CoA + choline → ACh.

**Major CNS sources**:
1. **Basal forebrain cholinergic nuclei (Ch1–Ch4)**: Nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM, → cortex), medial septum (MS, → hippocampus), diagonal band of Broca (DBB), substantia innominata. Provide ascending modulatory system for cortical arousal and cognition.
2. **Mesopontine tegmentum**: Pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN) and laterodorsal tegmentum (LDT) — project to thalamus, midbrain, basal forebrain; critical for REM sleep and arousal.
3. **Striatal interneurons**: tonically active cholinergic interneurons (~2% of striatal cells; enormous influence).

Peripheral: Motor neurons at NMJ; preganglionic neurons (sympathetic and parasympathetic ANS); parasympathetic postganglionic neurons.

### Where Stored
Packed into small **clear synaptic vesicles** (not dense-core) by **vesicular ACh transporter (VAChT)**.

### Release Pattern
- **CNS**: Both tonic (low-level during wakefulness) and phasic (transient bursts during attention, reward prediction, sensory events). Highest during wakefulness and REM sleep; lowest during NREM.
- **Peripheral/NMJ**: Phasic, action-potential-triggered.

### Units of Measurement
- CSF: **pmol/mL**
- Brain tissue: **nmol/g**
- Extracellular (microdialysis): **nM**

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

| Compartment | Concentration |
|---|---|
| Brain tissue — striatum (rat) | ~60–70 nmol/g wet tissue |
| Brain tissue — cortex (rat) | ~24 nmol/g wet tissue |
| Brain tissue — hippocampus (rat) | ~20–22 nmol/g wet tissue |
| CSF (human, HPLC/ECD) | ~6.14 ± 1.39 pmol/mL |
| Extracellular brain (microdialysis) | Low nM range |

*ACh is challenging to measure: extremely rapid AChE degradation makes static sampling difficult. No-net-flux microdialysis is the gold standard.*

### Receptors

**Nicotinic ACh receptors (nAChRs) — ionotropic, ligand-gated cation channels (Na⁺/K⁺/Ca²⁺)**:
| Subtype | Location | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **α4β2** | Thalamus (most abundant CNS nAChR), basal forebrain (NBM) | Nicotine's attention-enhancing and mood effects |
| **α7** | Hippocampus (CA1–CA3, DG), thalamus, PFC | High Ca²⁺ permeability; LTP, memory consolidation, anti-inflammatory signaling |
| α3β4, etc. | Autonomic ganglia, peripheral NMJ | Autonomic transmission |

**Muscarinic ACh receptors (mAChRs) — metabotropic GPCRs**:
| Receptor | Coupling | Location | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **M1** | Gq | Hippocampus, cortex, striatum (~35–60% of hippocampal/cortical mAChRs) | Critical for cognitive function, LTP induction, working memory; key Alzheimer's target |
| **M2** | Gi | Presynaptic autoreceptor on cholinergic terminals; heart (vagal) | Inhibits ACh release; cardiac slowing |
| M3 | Gq | Smooth muscle, glands (peripheral); some CNS | Secretion, smooth muscle contraction |
| M4 | Gi | Striatum | Modulates dopamine release |
| M5 | Gq | VTA, substantia nigra (dopamine neuron-associated) | Facilitates dopamine reward; drug reinforcement |

### Receptor Sensitivity and Habituation
- M1/α4β2: downregulate with chronic nicotine/cholinergic activation — tolerance to cognitive effects.
- **Alzheimer's disease**: dramatic loss of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons → reduced ACh → downregulated M1 and α4β2. Basis of cholinergic hypothesis. Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine): prolong ACh availability.
- M1 receptor facilitates NMDA receptor-dependent plasticity for LTP in hippocampus.

### CNS vs. Peripheral
**Both**. CNS: modulates cortical arousal, attention, memory, REM sleep. Peripheral: NMJ (all voluntary movement), entire ANS (all preganglionic; parasympathetic postganglionic).

### Reuptake and Degradation
**Degradation is primary** (unlike most neurotransmitters where reuptake dominates):
- **Acetylcholinesterase (AChE)**: concentrated in synaptic cleft; hydrolyzes ACh → choline + acetic acid in **microseconds to milliseconds**. Rate: ~25,000 molecules/second per enzyme molecule (one of the fastest enzymatic reactions known).
- **Butyrylcholinesterase (BuChE)**: broader distribution including plasma; slower.
- **Choline recovery**: high-affinity choline transporter (ChT) in presynaptic terminal recycles ~50% of choline directly.

**Effective synaptic half-life**: <1 ms at NMJ; slightly longer in CNS.

### Key Brain Regions
Basal forebrain → cortex, hippocampus, amygdala; LDT/PPN → thalamus, midbrain, brainstem; striatal interneurons; entire PNS.

### Primary Effects
- Attention and arousal
- Memory encoding and consolidation (hippocampal M1 and α7 nAChRs)
- REM sleep (ACh from LDT/PPN drives REM; highest ACh tone during REM)
- Cortical signal-to-noise enhancement (suppresses "background" processing, sharpens salient responses)
- Reward modulation (M5 on dopamine neurons)
- Motor control (NMJ); heart rate (M2, vagal); glandular secretion (M3)

### High ACh Phenotype
Hyper-alertness, vivid imagery, sharpened perception, accelerated memory encoding. During REM sleep: the dreaming state. Cholinomimetic overdose (organophosphate poisoning): confusion, hypersalivation, bradycardia, seizures.

### Low ACh Phenotype
Cognitive fog, poor attention, memory encoding failures, drowsiness (NREM-like state). The "cotton brain" of Alzheimer's. Anticholinergic drugs (diphenhydramine, scopolamine) → confusion, amnesia, sedation, delirium at high doses.

## ANANDAMIDE (N-arachidonoylethanolamine / AEA)

### Class and Type
**Endocannabinoid lipid mediator**. Arachidonic acid-derived fatty acid amide. Named from Sanskrit "ananda" (bliss). The "bliss molecule."

### Where Synthesized / Produced
Synthesized **on demand** in the postsynaptic neuron in response to Ca²⁺ influx and/or metabotropic receptor activation. **Not stored.**

**Primary pathway**: N-arachidonoyl phosphatidylethanolamine (NAPE, a membrane phospholipid precursor) is cleaved by **NAPE-specific phospholipase D (NAPE-PLD)** to release anandamide. Secondary pathways exist (NAPE-PLD knockout mice still produce AEA).

**Activity-dependent**: Depolarization of postsynaptic cell → Ca²⁺ influx → NAPE-PLD activation → AEA synthesis from membrane lipids → retrograde release.

### Where Stored
**None.** Synthesized on demand from membrane lipid precursors. No storage vesicle. Defining retrograde signaling feature of endocannabinoids.

### Release Pattern
**Phasic, demand-driven**: AEA produced in bursts following significant postsynaptic activity or stress. Unlike 2-AG (the other major endocannabinoid, more abundantly produced), AEA has a more complex tonic-modulatory role that varies by region.

**Retrograde signaling**: Postsynaptic cell fires → Ca²⁺ → AEA synthesized → diffuses backward across synapse → binds CB1 on presynaptic terminal → reduces neurotransmitter release. This is "**depolarization-induced suppression of excitation (DSE)**" or inhibition (DSI). Acts as a brake on glutamate and GABA release depending on context.

### Units of Measurement
- **pg/mL** or **nM** (plasma); **ng/mL** (common in exercise studies)

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

| Compartment | Concentration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Plasma (healthy humans, resting) | ~0.58 ± 0.21 ng/mL ≈ **~1.9 nM** | Extremely labile; requires immediate processing |
| Plasma (acute schizophrenia) | ~7.79 pmol/mL | Elevated ~3× vs. controls |
| Brain tissue | pmol/g range | Very low; rapid degradation makes measurement difficult |
| Post-exercise plasma (15 min post-run) | ~0.57 ± 0.17 ng/mL | Modest increase; modest elevation vs. rest |

### Receptors

| Receptor | Type | Location | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CB1** | Gi/o-coupled GPCR | Presynaptic; **cortex, hippocampus, basal ganglia (caudate-putamen, SNc), cerebellum, amygdala** | Primary CNS target; suppresses presynaptic neurotransmitter release; AEA is a **partial agonist** (lower efficacy than THC) |
| CB2 | Gi/o-coupled | Peripheral immune cells (macrophages, B-cells), microglia | Peripheral inflammation; AEA nearly inactive here |
| **TRPV1** | Ion channel (capsaicin receptor) | Sensory neurons, CNS | AEA is an endogenous TRPV1 agonist → pain modulation via non-CB pathway |
| PPARα/PPARγ | Nuclear receptors | Ubiquitous | Anti-inflammatory effects |

### Receptor Sensitivity and Desensitization
- **FAAH expression** reduced ~14–20% in regular cannabis users (adaptive downregulation compensating for exogenous THC).
- CB1 receptor: undergoes desensitization/internalization with sustained agonist exposure. Chronic cannabis use → CB1 downregulation → tolerance (reduced effect from equivalent THC dose).

### CNS vs. Peripheral
**Both**. CB1 primarily CNS; CB2 + peripheral TRPV1 provide peripheral roles.

### Degradation and Half-Life
- **FAAH (Fatty Acid Amide Hydrolase)**: Primary degradative enzyme; membrane-bound, intracellular (postsynaptic). Converts AEA → arachidonic acid + ethanolamine. Highly efficient — keeps basal AEA very low.
- **Cellular uptake**: AEA taken up via putative membrane transporter (identity debated) before FAAH degradation.
- **Half-life**: **Seconds to minutes** in vivo. FAAH knockout mice have dramatically elevated brain AEA, confirming FAAH as the rate-limiting step.

**Runner's High**: Sustained aerobic exercise (>30 min at moderate-high intensity) elevates plasma AEA. Current evidence suggests the runner's high is more **endocannabinoid-mediated** than endorphin-mediated (endorphins cannot cross the BBB efficiently; AEA can).

### Key Brain Regions
Amygdala, hippocampus, PFC, hypothalamus, basal ganglia, nucleus accumbens, cerebellum, PAG.

### Primary Effects
- Mild euphoria, hedonic tone, sense of wellbeing
- Pain modulation (central CB1 and peripheral TRPV1/CB2)
- Appetite regulation (CB1 in hypothalamus and limbic system)
- Anxiolysis (CB1 in amygdala suppresses fear)
- Memory modulation — facilitates forgetting of aversive memories (hippocampal CB1)
- Reward modulation via dopamine interaction in NAc

### High AEA Phenotype
Mild euphoria, anxiolysis, sensory enhancement, reduced pain sensitivity, increased appetite. The "bliss" of intense exercise. Quality similar to mild cannabis but far milder in practice. FAAH inhibitor drugs produce anxiolytic/antidepressant-like effects in animal models.

### Low AEA Phenotype
Baseline or below-baseline mood, lower stress resilience, heightened pain sensitivity, anxiety. Deficient endocannabinoid tone is associated with PTSD, anxiety disorders, depression, and IBS ("clinical endocannabinoid deficiency" hypothesis).

## SUBSTANCE P

### Class and Type
**Neuropeptide**; 11-amino acid peptide; member of the tachykinin family. Encoded by the TAC1 gene. Acts as neurotransmitter/neuromodulator and as immune/inflammatory mediator.

### Where Synthesized
- **Primary afferent (sensory) neurons** in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and trigeminal ganglia — small C fibers and Aδ fibers (nociceptors).
- **CNS neurons**: hypothalamus, amygdala, substantia nigra, PAG, habenula, striatum, brainstem raphe nuclei.
- **Immune cells**: mast cells, macrophages, eosinophils, lymphocytes.

Synthesized from preproTAC1 precursor → proSubstance P → Substance P (11 AA) via proteolytic processing.

### Where Stored
LDCVs in nerve terminals — distinct from small clear vesicles of classical fast neurotransmitters. Transported via fast axonal transport from cell body to both central (spinal dorsal horn) and peripheral terminal endings.

### Release Pattern
**Activity-dependent, high-threshold phasic**: Requires **intense or sustained noxious stimulation** to trigger release. Routine somatosensory input does not release SP. High-frequency nociceptor activity or tissue damage triggers LDCV exocytosis. SP release is slower-onset and longer-duration than classical fast transmitters — acts as a **volume transmitter** diffusing beyond the synapse.

### Units of Measurement
- Plasma, CSF, serum: **pg/mL**
- Tissue: **fmol/mg**

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

| Compartment | Concentration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Plasma (healthy humans) | ~132–458 pg/mL (mean ~217 pg/mL) | — |
| CSF (healthy controls) | Low pg/mL range | Highly variable by assay |
| CSF (major depression) | Significantly elevated vs. controls | — |
| CSF (PTSD) | Elevated ~55–169% above healthy controls | — |
| CSF (fibromyalgia) | Elevated ~3× above healthy controls | Most FM patients above normal ceiling |

### Receptors

| Receptor | Coupling | Location | Key Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NK1 (neurokinin 1 / SP receptor)** | Gq → PLC → IP3/DAG → Ca²⁺ + PKC | **Spinal cord lamina I** (dense; activates spinal pain-relay neurons); hypothalamus, amygdala, striatum, PAG, BNST, NTS | **Primary and highest-affinity target** for SP; mediates "wind-up" central sensitization |
| NK2 | — | — | Lower affinity for SP; more responsive to neurokinin A |
| NK3 | — | — | More responsive to neurokinin B |

**NK1 endocytosis**: Receptor internalizes following sustained SP stimulation — a cellular marker of nociceptor activation and pain chronicity.

### Receptor Dynamics (Wind-Up / Central Sensitization)
Repeated C-fiber activation releases both SP and glutamate. SP-mediated NK1 activation + NMDA receptor activation → temporal summation and progressive amplification of pain signals (central sensitization). Mechanism of **chronic pain hypersensitivity**. SP acts as a slow, amplifying signal that compounds glutamate's fast excitation.

### CNS vs. Peripheral
**Both**. CNS: emotional processing, stress responses, pain modulation. Peripheral: neurogenic inflammation, immune cell activation, mast cell degranulation.

### Degradation and Half-Life
No reuptake transporter. Degraded by extracellular peptidases:
- **Neutral endopeptidase (neprilysin)**: primary in tissue extracellular fluid.
- **Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)**: degrades SP in blood plasma.
- **Half-life**: seconds to a few minutes in extracellular fluid; minutes to hours in blood plasma.

### Key Brain Regions
Spinal cord dorsal horn (primary pain relay), amygdala, hypothalamus (PVN), PAG, habenula, BNST, brainstem raphe.

### Primary Effects
- Pain transmission periphery → spinal cord → brain
- Central sensitization / wind-up pain
- Neurogenic inflammation (triggers mast cell degranulation, vasodilation, plasma extravasation)
- Emotional stress responses
- Emesis (NK1 antagonists are antiemetics — aprepitant)
- Mood modulation (NK1 in limbic system; NK1 antagonists trialed as antidepressants)

### High Substance P Phenotype
Pain amplification, emotional distress, anxiety, nausea, "suffering" quality. SP mediates the feeling that pain is **terrible**, not merely present (amygdalar NK1 circuit). Elevated CSF SP found in depression, PTSD, fibromyalgia, and IBS.

### Low Substance P Phenotype
Reduced pain signaling, emotional blunting. NK1 antagonists show some benefit for emotional distress (disappointing for analgesia in clinical trials).

## NPY (NEUROPEPTIDE Y)

### Class and Type
36-amino acid **neuropeptide**; member of the pancreatic polypeptide family (with peptide YY/PYY and pancreatic polypeptide/PP). One of the most abundant neuropeptides in the mammalian CNS.

### Where Synthesized
From preproNPY (97 aa precursor) in:
- **Hypothalamus** (arcuate nucleus, PVN, dorsomedial nucleus — highest CNS concentrations)
- **Locus coeruleus** (colocalized with norepinephrine in ~70–80% of noradrenergic neurons)
- **Hippocampus** (dentate gyrus interneurons)
- **Amygdala**
- **Nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS)**, cortex, striatum, NAc
- **Peripheral**: sympathetic neurons (co-stored with NE and ATP), adrenal medulla, gut (enteric NS)

### Where Stored
LDCVs; often co-stored with classical monoamine transmitters (particularly NE in locus coeruleus neurons). Requires **higher stimulation frequencies** for LDCV exocytosis than co-stored small vesicle transmitters.

### Release Pattern
**Activity-dependent phasic**: NPY released at high firing frequencies (>5–10 Hz). Acute stress triggers NPY release from locus coeruleus, sympathetic terminals, and hypothalamus. Cortisol and NPY **co-rise** during acute stress (positive covariation).

### Units of Measurement
- CSF and plasma: **pg/mL**

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

| Compartment | Concentration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| CSF (healthy controls) | ~360 ± 17.7 pg/mL (one assay; ~792 pg/mL by other assays) | Methodological variation significant |
| Plasma (healthy, resting) | ~220 pg/mL | — |
| CSF (PTSD) | ~233.6 ± 28.7 pg/mL | Significantly **reduced** vs. controls |
| CSF (major depression) | Reduced vs. healthy controls | — |
| Plasma (military survival training, acute peak stress) | Elevated acutely, then depleted with chronic stress | — |

### Receptors
All Gi/Go-coupled GPCRs → inhibit adenylyl cyclase → reduce cAMP.

| Receptor | Primary Role | Location |
|---|---|---|
| **Y1** | Postsynaptic; **anxiolytic** (Y1 agonism reduces anxiety); feeding stimulation | Hypothalamus, hippocampus, neocortex, thalamus |
| **Y2** | Presynaptic autoreceptor (feedback inhibition of NPY release); modulates glutamate/GABA | Hippocampus, thalamus, hypothalamus |
| Y4 | Satiety (responds preferentially to PP); appetite inhibition | — |
| **Y5** | Hippocampus, hypothalamus; **strongest feeding stimulation** (most potent NPY-driven appetite signal); anticonvulsant | Hippocampus, hypothalamus |

**Functional role summary**: Y1/Y5 → appetite stimulation; Y2/Y4 → satiety/appetite inhibition; Y1/Y2 → anxiolysis; all subtypes contribute to stress resilience.

### Relationship to Cortisol and Ghrelin
- **Cortisol**: During acute stress, cortisol and NPY rise together. NPY acts as a stress buffer, dampening the emotional impact of cortisol-driven arousal.
- **Ghrelin**: Elevated cortisol increases peripheral acyl-ghrelin. Ghrelin acts at hypothalamic PVN to stimulate NPY release → modulates CRH/ACTH/cortisol output (a feedback loop linking metabolic state, hunger, and stress).
- Higher NPY response to stress correlates with fewer dissociative symptoms and less psychological distress — a **PTSD resilience factor**.

### CNS vs. Peripheral
**Both**. CNS: stress resilience, anxiety suppression, appetite control, anticonvulsant. Peripheral: sympathetic co-transmitter (vasoconstriction), gut motility, adipose tissue metabolism, cardiac regulation.

### Degradation and Half-Life
- Degraded extracellularly by **neutral endopeptidase (neprilysin)** and **DPP-IV** (cleaves N-terminal Tyr-Pro → generates NPY(3–36) with altered receptor affinity).
- **Half-life**: minutes in tissue; longer in plasma. More stable than Substance P due to different peptide structure.
- No reuptake transporter.

### Key Brain Regions
Hypothalamus (arcuate — appetite), hippocampus (anxiety, memory, anticonvulsant), amygdala (fear/anxiety modulation), locus coeruleus (co-release with NE during stress).

### Primary Effects
- **Appetite stimulation** (most potent orexigenic agent in the hypothalamus)
- **Anxiolysis** and stress resilience
- **Anticonvulsant** (Y2/Y5 mediated)
- **Antidepressant-like** (low NPY correlates with depression)
- **Vasoconstrictive** in periphery (sympathetic co-release with NE)
- **NREM sleep promotion**
- Blunts physiological and subjective stress response

### High NPY Phenotype
Hunger (orexigenic signal), emotional calm under stress, psychological resilience, blunted fear. Elite soldiers with high NPY responsivity show lower PTSD incidence. Neuroprotective buffer against chronic stress.

### Low NPY Phenotype
Anxiety, heightened stress reactivity, depression, increased fear, poor resilience to trauma, increased seizure susceptibility. Low CSF NPY is a consistent biomarker in PTSD, depression. Paradoxically **elevated** in anorexia nervosa (body compensating for starvation).

## CRH (CORTICOTROPIN-RELEASING HORMONE)

### Class and Type
41-amino acid **neuropeptide hormone**. Also called CRF (corticotropin-releasing factor). The **master regulator of the HPA axis** and a key orchestrator of the brain's response to stress and threat.

### Where Synthesized
From pre-proCRH (196 aa) → proCRH → CRH (41 aa):

**Primary sources**:
- **Hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN)**: The classical HPA-axis source. Parvocellular neurons release CRH into hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal blood → anterior pituitary. This is the HPA drive system.
- **Amygdala (central nucleus, CeA)**: Large CRH-expressing neuron population projecting to BNST, locus coeruleus, hippocampus, etc. The **extrahypothalamic CRH system** — responsible for the emotional/behavioral dimensions of stress (separate from HPA activation).
- **Bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST)**: Projects widely to arousal and anxiety circuits; mediates sustained/anticipatory anxiety.
- Cortex, hippocampus, LC, cerebellum, brainstem: additional CRH neurons.
- **Periphery**: Skin, gonads, immune cells (local paracrine roles during inflammation).

### Where Stored
LDCVs. Often co-stored with other peptides (e.g., arginine vasopressin/AVP in PVN, which potentiates CRH's ACTH-releasing action up to **10×** during chronic stress).

### Release Dynamics
- **Phasic + circadian**: CRH from PVN follows pulsatile, circadian-entrained pattern (peaks early morning aligned with cortisol awakening response).
- Acute stressors → rapid large PVN CRH burst → HPA cascade within minutes.
- Amygdala CRH: released with threat detection and conditioned fear.

**HPA cascade**: CRH (hypothalamus) → portal blood → anterior pituitary → ACTH release → adrenal cortex → **cortisol** (full cortisol peak: ~15–30 min after CRH).

### Units of Measurement
- **pg/mL** (CSF, plasma)
- Plasma: CRH-binding protein (CRH-BP) inactivates ~70–90% of circulating CRH → plasma measurement is poor indicator of central CRH tone. **CSF is the preferred measurement compartment**.

### Real-Life Concentration Ranges

| Compartment | Concentration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| CSF (healthy controls) | ~38–42 pg/mL (mean ~42.3 pg/mL, SD 15.6) | — |
| CSF (PTSD) | ~55.2 ± 16.4 pg/mL | Significantly elevated |
| CSF (major depression, melancholic) | Elevated in some studies; other studies show no difference | Inconsistent across literature |
| Plasma | Very low; rapidly cleared | Not the standard measurement |

### Receptors
Both Gs-coupled → stimulate adenylyl cyclase → ↑cAMP → PKA:

| Receptor | Affinity for CRH | Distribution | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CRF1 (CRH-R1)** | High affinity | **Neocortex, anterior pituitary** (mediates ACTH), **amygdala** (BLA), hippocampus, cerebellum | HPA activation, anxiety, fear, behavioral effects of acute stress |
| **CRF2 (CRH-R2)** | Lower affinity; responds more to urocortins | **Lateral septum, VMH, amygdala (medial), raphe nuclei, heart** | Stress **recovery** and appetite reduction; counterbalances CRF1 in some circuits |

**CRH-binding protein (CRH-BP)**: Decoy binder in blood and brain. Sequesters CRH, limiting receptor access. Acts as a buffer/regulator of CRH bioavailability.

### Regulation of CRH Release
**Stimulants**: Serotonin, ACh, IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, norepinephrine, CRH itself (feedforward at CeA), psychological stressors.

**Inhibitors**: GABA (major brake at PVN — loss of GABAergic inhibition disinhibits CRH in depression), glucocorticoids (negative feedback at PVN, hippocampus, pituitary), CRH-BP.

**PTSD vs. melancholic depression**:
- **PTSD**: Hyperactive central CRH (high CSF CRH) **with** suppressed HPA output (low cortisol) — paradox explained by glucocorticoid receptor sensitization (overshooting of negative feedback).
- **Melancholic depression**: Hyperactive central CRH **with** hyperactive HPA (high cortisol) — failure of negative feedback.

### CNS vs. Peripheral
**Both**. CNS: stress initiation, anxiety, fear, depression. Peripheral: inflammatory modulation in skin, reproductive organs.

### Degradation and Half-Life
- **Plasma half-life**: ~15 min.
- Degraded by plasma proteases. CRH-BP inactivates a large fraction in blood.
- No specific CNS reuptake transporter; degraded by extracellular peptidases.

### Key Brain Regions
**Hypothalamus (PVN)** — HPA master regulator; **amygdala (CeA)** — fear/anxiety/emotional stress; **BNST** — anticipatory anxiety ("sustained apprehension" vs. CeA's discrete fear); **locus coeruleus** — arousal, hypervigilance; **hippocampus** — stress-related memory and HPA negative feedback; **raphe nuclei** — interaction with serotonin.

### Primary Effects
- Initiates HPA axis (CRH → ACTH → cortisol cascade)
- Activates sympathetic NS via locus coeruleus (CRH drives LC/NE axis in parallel with HPA)
- Anxiety and fear (amygdala CRH neurons)
- Anorexia (suppresses appetite)
- Arousal and hypervigilance
- Memory encoding for emotional events (amygdala CRH facilitates consolidation of stressful experiences)
- Immune modulation (peripheral)

### High CRH Phenotype
**Acute**: Stress response — fight/flight arousal, anxiety, fear, hypervigilance, suppressed appetite, accelerated heart rate.
**Chronic**: Sustained anxiety, anticipatory dread, insomnia, cognitive intrusions, emotional hyperreactivity. Neurochemical signature of PTSD's hyperarousal and melancholic depression's agitated dysphoria.

### Low CRH / Blunted HPA Axis Phenotype
Fatigue, emotional flatness, difficulty mobilizing stress responses. Seen in burnout, atypical depression, adrenal insufficiency. Chronically overdriven CRH systems may eventually downregulate (CRF1 desensitization), contributing to the exhaustion phase of chronic stress.
